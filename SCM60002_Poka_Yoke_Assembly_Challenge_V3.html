SCM60002 · Poka‑Yoke Assembly Challenge
From “be careful” → inspection → mistake-proofing
Step 1
Welcome

Can the process help you avoid making a mistake?

You will assemble short customer orders under three different quality systems. The work is similar, but the system surrounding the worker changes.

Round 1 · No safeguardThe process accepts whatever you choose.
Round 2 · InspectionThe process checks the finished unit and sends errors back for rework.
Round 3 · Poka‑YokeThe process immediately blocks the wrong component before assembly.
Concept

What Poka‑Yoke is trying to change

1. “Be careful”The worker must remember everything correctly. The process itself offers little protection.
→
2. DetectInspection catches a defect after the mistake has already occurred.
→
3. PreventA fixture, sensor, software rule or design makes the wrong action impossible or immediately obvious.
Poka‑Yoke does not mean people are careless. It accepts that human errors can occur and asks whether the process can stop that error from becoming a defect.
Real examples: a plug that fits only one orientation; a barcode scanner that rejects the wrong component; a form that cannot be submitted while a required field is missing; a fixture that will not accept the wrong-sized part.

Each customer order contains a housing colour, insert shape, connector direction and cap size. The order is shown briefly, then hidden so the task still depends on human attention and memory.

There are 8 orders in each round. Do not worry if you make a mistake. The point of the exercise is to see how different process designs respond when a mistake occurs.
Important system test: On the first order of each round, deliberately click one wrong component once. In Round 1 and Round 2, the process will accept the selection. In Round 3, Poka‑Yoke will physically block it. This guarantees that you can actually see the difference between the systems.
Round 1 · No safeguard

The process accepts whatever you select

The order is visible for 2 seconds, then hidden. Assemble the product from memory. There is no inspection and no mistake-proofing.

Round 1 reflection

“Be careful” is not a process control

The process allowed the wrong action

Even if you personally completed every counted order correctly, you saw during the system test that an incorrect component could be selected and accepted.

That means quality depends heavily on attention, memory and perfect execution.

Lean question: instead of repeatedly telling people to “be more careful”, can we redesign the process so the mistake is harder to make?
  • Mistakes can pass downstream.
  • Errors may become customer defects.
  • The system gives no immediate feedback.
  • Training alone cannot eliminate human error.
Round 2 · Final inspection

Detect the mistake before shipment

The assembly task is the same, but now every completed unit is inspected. Wrong units cannot reach the customer—but they must be corrected.

Round 2 reflection

Inspection protects the customer—but creates rework

Detection is better than shipping the defect

Inspection catches an incorrect unit before it reaches the customer. But the mistake has already happened: the product must be checked, diagnosed and corrected.

This is the distinction: inspection detects a defect; Poka‑Yoke tries to prevent the mistake from becoming a defect in the first place.
  • Customer defects can fall.
  • Inspection itself takes effort.
  • Incorrect units require rework.
  • Waste has already occurred.
Round 3 · Poka‑Yoke

The wrong component cannot proceed

The order is still shown briefly and then hidden. But now the assembly fixture “knows” the specification. A wrong component is rejected immediately; a correct component locks into place.

Conclusion

Poka‑Yoke builds quality into the process

The objective is not to create a worker who never makes a mistake. The objective is to create a process in which a normal human mistake is prevented, constrained, or immediately detected before it becomes a defect.

  • No safeguard: wrong actions are possible and may become customer defects.
  • Inspection: defects are detected after assembly, so customer protection improves but rework remains.
  • Poka‑Yoke: the incorrect action is blocked at the source; the mistake does not proceed into the process.
  • Lean connection: this reduces defects, rework, inspection burden, wasted material, waiting and downstream disruption.
  • Management lesson: improve the system instead of relying only on instructions such as “be more careful”.
One sentence to remember: Do not only detect defects after they happen—design the process so the mistake cannot easily become a defect.
